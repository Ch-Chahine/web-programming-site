History of the Internet

The Internet is a system that connects computers and networks and allows them to communicate. It developed from early research networks such as ARPANET and expanded through technologies like TCP/IP. It grew from a research network into the worldwide Internet we use today.

Important Milestones

1969 — ARPANET

What happened: ARPANET began with four computer nodes. The first computer-to-computer signal was sent between UCLA and the Stanford Research Institute on October 29, 1969.

Why it was important: ARPANET was one of the earliest computer networks and became an important foundation for the development of the Internet.

Source: DARPA

1971 — Networked Email

What happened: Ray Tomlinson successfully sent a networked message from one computer to another in late 1971 using ARPANET.

Why it was important: Email became an important way for people to communicate through computer networks.

Source: Computer History Museum

1973 — International ARPANET Connections

What happened: ARPANET expanded internationally through a satellite connection that linked the network to sites in Norway and the United Kingdom.

Why it was important: This extended ARPANET beyond the United States and was an important step toward the development of international computer networking.

Source: Computer History Museum

1973–1974 — Development of TCP

What happened: Bob Kahn and Vint Cerf worked on a protocol for connecting different computer networks. They presented their TCP ideas in 1973 and published their work in 1974.

Why it was important: Their work later developed into TCP/IP, which became a fundamental technology that allows different networks to communicate over the Internet.

Source: Computer History Museum

1983 — ARPANET Transition to TCP/IP

What happened: On January 1, 1983, ARPANET changed its host communication protocol from NCP to TCP/IP.

Why it was important: The transition established TCP/IP as the common protocol for communication across ARPANET and was a major step toward the development of the modern Internet.

Source: Internet Society

1983–1984 — Introduction and Development of DNS

What happened: In 1983, Paul Mockapetris published RFC 882 and RFC 883, which defined the Domain Name System (DNS). In 1984, the domain structure was further developed with top-level domains such as .com, .edu, .gov, .mil, and .org.

Why it was important: DNS provided a scalable way to use domain names instead of relying only on numerical Internet addresses, making the growing Internet easier to organize and use.

Source: Internet Society

1986 — NSFNET Was Launched

What happened: The U.S. National Science Foundation launched NSFNET in 1986 to connect academic researchers to a system of supercomputer centers.

Why it was important: NSFNET expanded access to computer networking for researchers and became the main U.S. Internet backbone, helping the Internet grow significantly.

Source: National Science Foundation

1990 — ARPANET Was Decommissioned

What happened: The original ARPANET was formally decommissioned in 1990.

Why it was important: The shutdown marked the end of the original ARPANET, while the Internet had already developed into a larger system of interconnected networks.

Source: DARPA

1995 — NSFNET Backbone Was Retired

What happened: The National Science Foundation shut down its dedicated Internet backbone infrastructure in 1995 as commercial Internet services expanded rapidly.

Why it was important: This marked an important transition from a government-supported Internet backbone toward privately operated and commercial Internet infrastructure.

Source: National Science Foundation

2000s — Growth of Broadband and Wi-Fi

What happened: During the 2000s, broadband Internet and wireless Internet access became much more widespread. By 2009, 63% of American adults had broadband at home, and 83% of laptop owners connected using Wi-Fi.

Why it was important: Broadband provided faster Internet connections, while Wi-Fi made it possible to connect devices to the Internet wirelessly, making Internet access faster and more convenient.

Broadband Source: Pew Research Center
Wi-Fi Source: Pew Research Center

2000s–2010s — Growth of Mobile Internet

What happened: Mobile Internet grew rapidly during the 2000s and 2010s. By 2015, global mobile-broadband penetration reached 47%, which was 12 times higher than in 2007.

Why it was important: Mobile broadband allowed people to access the Internet while away from fixed connections, making Internet access more mobile and widely available.

Source: International Telecommunication Union

2010s–2020s — Expansion of Fiber, 4G and 5G

What happened: Fiber-optic broadband and advanced mobile networks expanded significantly. By the end of 2023, fiber accounted for 42% of fixed broadband subscriptions in OECD countries. By 2024, 4G covered 92% of the world's population and 5G covered 51%.

Why it was important: These technologies provided faster connections, greater network capacity, and improved access to high-speed mobile Internet.

Fiber Source: OECD
4G and 5G Source: International Telecommunication Union

Key Technologies in the Development of the Internet

Packet Switching

What it is: Packet switching divides data into smaller packets that travel through a network and are reassembled when they reach their destination.

Why it was important: It allowed data to travel through different paths in a network without depending on a single node, making it a fundamental technology used in ARPANET and Internet development.

Source: DARPA

TCP/IP (Transmission Control Protocol / Internet Protocol)

What it is: TCP/IP is a set of communication protocols that allows computers and different networks to communicate with each other over the Internet.

Why it was important: TCP/IP made it possible to interconnect different networks using common communication rules. ARPANET's transition to TCP/IP in 1983 was a major step in the development of the modern Internet.

Source: Internet Society

DNS (Domain Name System)

What it is: DNS is a distributed system that connects human-readable domain names to the numerical IP addresses used by computers.

Why it was important: DNS made the growing Internet easier to organize and use because people could use domain names instead of having to remember numerical IP addresses.

Source: Internet Society
